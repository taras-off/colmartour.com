Little Venice in Colmar: canals, quarters, and how to see them

The Lauch River, Fishermen's Quay, the Tanners' Quarter, and boat rides past the market gardens.

By Eugene · Updated October 2026

Colmar audio guide — €9.99 Stay in Colmar

Colorful facades above the Lauch River make up one of Colmar's best-known views. Fishermen, boatmen, tanners, and market gardeners once lived and worked here, and traces of their trades are still easy to spot, from the name of a quay to the drying lofts under the roofs.

What is Little Venice?

Little Venice (Petite Venise) is the stretch of the Lauch where the houses stand right along the water. It begins behind the Koïfhus, runs past Fishermen's Quay, and continues to the Turenne and Saint-Pierre bridges.

The area is small, and you can see it and the neighboring quarters in a single walk.

Historic quarters by the water

Fishermen's Quay

Today, Fishermen's Quay (Quai de la Poissonnerie) is lined with restaurants and winstubs, Alsace's traditional wine taverns, but its name hints at what it used to be. Professional fishermen lived here and, together with the boatmen, formed a single guild. They kept their catch in cages in the river and sold it right on the quay. So when you sit down to an Alsatian meal here, you're just steps from where the fish trade once took place.

Tanners' Quarter

The first thing you'll notice in the Tanners' Quarter (Quartier des Tanneurs) is its tall, narrow half-timbered houses from the 17th and 18th centuries. Look up. The tanners dried hides on the top floors, which were often open to the air, and that trace of the trade still shows in the shape of the houses.

The quarter owes much of its present appearance to a major restoration carried out between 1968 and 1974.

Market gardeners' quarter (Krutenau)

Past Fishermen's Quay, things get quieter and the banks turn greener. This is where the Krutenau begins. Winegrowers, market gardeners, and boatmen lived here, and even the quarter's name comes from the vegetable gardens on the edge of town. The green banks are best seen from a boat.

The quarter's main street, Rue de Turenne, has its own story. It's named after Marshal Turenne, who made a ceremonial entry into the town along this street in 1674.

A historical plaque near the bridge over the Lauch tells of Martin Stockmeyer, a local boatman nicknamed "the Hercules of Colmar". Why he earned that nickname is one of the stories in the TouringBee audio guide.

A walk along the water

Little Venice is easy to explore on foot, starting from the square by the Koïfhus, where it begins. The walk takes you past the tall tanners' houses, then out onto Fishermen's Quay with its colorful facades, and on to Rue de Turenne. The classic shot of the area is from the Little Venice bridge (Pont de l'Abreuvoir), where you can see the houses standing right over the water.

Keep going over the bridge across the Lauch to Rue de la Herse, and the crowds thin out noticeably. From here, pedestrian streets along the river lead to the Saint-Pierre Bridge; a map makes the way easier to follow. From this bridge, you see Little Venice from the south, and down below, at the landing, the boats wait to set off.

Nearby, on Place des Six Montagnes Noires, stands the Roesselmann Fountain. It's the work of Auguste Bartholdi, a Colmar native and the sculptor of the Statue of Liberty. You'll find another Bartholdi piece at the covered market.

Things to do along the canals

Boat ride. From the water, Little Venice looks different than it does from the bridges. The flat-bottomed boat glides past the same houses and Fishermen's Quay, then heads into the quiet, green market gardeners' quarter. The ride takes about 25 minutes.

Boats leave from the landing at the foot of the Saint-Pierre Bridge, next to the Le Caveau Saint-Pierre restaurant. You can walk down to it from Rue de la Herse or Boulevard Saint-Pierre. Strollers can be left on the pontoon, and one ride is included in the City Pass. The schedule is seasonal, so check the operator's calendar before you go.

Covered market. The market's story begins on the water. Market gardeners from the neighboring quarter once brought their produce into town along the Lauch in flat-bottomed boats, and the market still stands by the river, at the edge of Little Venice. The brick-and-stone building with a metal frame went up in 1865. After a restoration, it reopened as a permanent covered market in 2010.

Today, people come here for goods from local producers, Alsatian specialties among them, or simply for a bite between walks.

Before you leave, look for the Little Winegrower (Petit Vigneron) in a niche at the corner of the building. The statue at the market is a copy; the original is in the Bartholdi Museum.

The market is closed on Mondays and open until 2 p.m. on Sundays.

Natural History and Ethnography Museum. In a natural history museum, you might expect mounted animals, minerals, and fossils, and you'll find them here. But the museum on Rue de Turenne goes further, with ethnographic collections and a section on ancient Egypt. Its collections are linked to a scientific society founded in 1859. Hours differ between school vacations and the rest of the year, there's a midday break, and the museum is closed on some holidays, so check the schedule before you go.

Evenings and the Christmas season

It's worth coming back to the water after dark. Since 1997, Colmar has run an architectural lighting program that won a special prize from the Académie des Arts de la Rue. On those evenings, the historic facades are lit up, and a walk here pairs nicely with dinner at a winstub on the quay.

During the Christmas season, Little Venice hosts the Children's Christmas Market (Marché de Noël des Enfants), one of Colmar's Christmas markets. And on some evenings, the tourist office puts on "Les enfants chantent Noël sur les barques" ("Children Sing Christmas on the Boats"): illuminated boats carrying children's choirs head out onto the water by the Turenne Bridge and Fishermen's Quay, and the kids sing carols right from the boats. Listening is free.

The lighting usually comes on Fridays and Saturdays, and on other evenings during major city events, so check the schedule before planning an evening walk. The boat concerts take place in late November and December, around 5 p.m., and only when the weather allows. Check the dates and times for both the market and the choirs before your trip.

Practical tips

  • When to go. You can stroll the quays anytime, but check the schedules for the boats, the market, the museum, and seasonal events separately. For photos, early morning or late afternoon works best. Boats run on a seasonal schedule, with fewer departures in the colder months, so check the operator's calendar ahead of time.
  • Skipping the boat? The classic views are from the Little Venice bridge and the Saint-Pierre Bridge, and the area is easy to cover on foot without rushing. A boat ride gives you a new view of the facades and takes you through the green market gardeners' quarter.
  • Combining it with the center. Little Venice and the neighboring quarters are in the southern part of the historic center, so they fit easily into a walk around the old town, home to the House of Heads, Pfister House, and other top sights. If you only have one day in Colmar, Little Venice is a good place to save for the evening.
