Why visit Colmar?

Because it survived. Two world wars rolled over Alsace and somehow left Colmar's centre almost untouched — which is why the old town still looks like an illustration rather than a reconstruction. The painted half-timbered houses aren't a heritage-district pastiche. They're the originals, lived in, with laundry in the windows.

It's also small. You can walk the whole historic centre in ninety minutes, which makes it one of the rare French towns you can genuinely see in a day. Our 27-stop audio walk is built around exactly that: a loop that hits everything worth stopping for and ends where you'd want to end anyway, on the water in Little Venice.

The story in five minutes

In 1226 Colmar became a free imperial city — answerable to the emperor and nobody else. No duke, no bishop, no local lord taking a cut. It minted its own currency. The bishop over in Strasbourg took this badly, and in 1262 sent troops. Colmar's council president, Johannes Roesselmann, died defending the walls; his body was smuggled home in a barrel so the besiegers couldn't desecrate it. He's the bronze figure on the fountain today — sculpted, like half of Colmar's public art, by a local boy called Bartholdi.

That would be Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi. Born here in 1834, on the street now named after him, in the house that's now his museum. He went on to build the Statue of Liberty. Colmar has a scale replica at the northern entrance to town, which surprises a lot of American visitors who came for the gingerbread houses.

The independence habit outlasted the empire. Colmar joined the Décapole, a league of Alsatian towns that banded together against anyone trying to tax them — a structure that looks a lot like Switzerland's, and that tells you most of what you need to know about the Alsatian temperament. French rule, German rule, French again, German again, French again: the town changed nationality four times in seventy-five years and kept ordering beer with its wine list throughout.

What you'll discover on the walk

Six of the twenty-seven stops.

La Petite Venise

The canal quarter the postcards are made of. Flat-bottomed boats, geraniums, and the tanners' houses with their open top floors — built that way to dry hides.

Maison Pfister & the painted houses

1537, and still the most photographed façade in Alsace. Next door, the Maison des Têtes wears 105 carved faces.

The Christmas markets

Six separate markets across the old town, 23 November to 29 December. The walk ends inside one of them in December.

The Marché Couvert

An 1865 iron-and-brick hall that's still a working market. Cheese, charcuterie, Alsatian wine by the glass at 10am, nobody judging.

The tanners' quarter

Wooden top floors left open to dry hides, and the Quai de la Poissonnerie behind them. A short walk on to the Koïfhus, where the Décapole met and goods were taxed.

Rue des Marchands at dusk

Bartholdi was born at number 30. Somewhere along here is also the smallest house in Colmar — barely wider than its own door, for reasons involving medieval property tax.

Almost everything that makes Colmar worth the trip costs nothing. The old town, the canals, the market squares, the façades — all free, all open, all the time. What you pay for is the inside of things: the Unterlinden, the Bartholdi, a boat on the canal, and whatever you drink.

The one ticket worth buying in advance

The Musée Unterlinden holds the Isenheim Altarpiece — a folding polyptych that is, without much competition, the most extraordinary painting in eastern France. €14, closed Tuesdays, last entry 17:30. In December the queue is real; book ahead.

Getting here by train

Colmar sits on the Paris–Strasbourg–Basel line, and the station is a ten-minute walk from the old town. Paris Gare de l'Est to Colmar is about 2h20 direct; from Strasbourg it's half an hour and trains run all day. Booking early is where the savings are.

Driving the Wine Route

Eguisheim, Riquewihr, Kaysersberg and Turckheim are all inside a 20-minute radius. Buses exist but they're sparse; a car turns a frustrating day into an easy one. (Turckheim is the exception — it has a station nine minutes from Colmar.)

Frequently asked questions

Is Colmar worth visiting?

Yes, with one caveat: it's small. You can see the historic centre properly in half a day, so build the rest of the trip around it — the wine villages, Strasbourg, or a slow afternoon on the canal. As a standalone three-day destination it will run out before you do.

How much does it cost to visit Colmar?

The old town is free — streets, canals, squares, façades, all of it. You pay for the Musée Unterlinden (€14), the Musée Bartholdi, a boat ride in Little Venice, and food. A full day including one museum and lunch runs roughly €40–60 per person.

When are the Colmar Christmas markets in 2026?

23 November to 29 December 2026, across six separate markets in the old town. Weekends in December are extremely busy; a weekday morning is a different experience entirely.

How do I get to Colmar from Paris?

Direct TGV from Paris Gare de l'Est, about 2h20. From Strasbourg it's roughly 30 minutes, from Basel about 45. The station is a ten-minute walk from the old town, so no onward transport is needed.

Do I need a car for the Alsace Wine Route?

It helps a great deal. Eguisheim, Riquewihr and Kaysersberg are all within 20 minutes of Colmar by road but poorly served by bus. Turckheim is the exception — nine minutes by train. Otherwise, hire a car or take a day tour.

Does the audio guide work without internet?

Yes. Download it over Wi-Fi before you set out and it runs completely offline — no data, no roaming. The offline GPS map shows where you are; you start each track yourself when you reach the stop.

What languages is the Colmar audio guide available in?

English, French, German, Italian and Polish. You choose the language when you buy.

How long does the audio walk take?

About an hour and a half of walking and listening, covering 27 stops. In practice most people take two to three hours because they stop for coffee, photographs, or a boat.
